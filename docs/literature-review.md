# Literature Review: Foundations, Explainability, and Evaluation for CareerInsight AI

## Scope and interpretive caution

This review synthesizes five independently verified sources relevant to the design of CareerInsight AI. The sources span a career-path recommender, a comparative university-major guidance study, explainable-recommender research, and broader recommender-evaluation literature. They provide methodological precedents and evaluation cautions; **they do not validate CareerInsight AI’s scores, weights, recommendations, outcomes, or performance**. In particular, none of the sources tests this prototype, its evidence schema, or its scoring rules.

## Career-recommender foundations

Qamhieh, Sammaneh, and Demaidi present the closest direct methodological precedent: PCRS, a personalized engineering-discipline recommender implemented as a mobile application. They derived personality-type/discipline associations from questionnaire data collected from 1,257 volunteer engineering students and graduates at one university, then combined academic performance with a personal-profile score incorporating personality and extracurricular activities. A Mamdani fuzzy-inference system with 15 if–then rules ranked seven engineering disciplines [1]. This design is relevant to CareerInsight AI because it demonstrates how multiple structured inputs can be made operational in a ranked suitability output, while also making a case for exposing the evidence-to-rule-to-score path rather than presenting an opaque recommendation.

PCRS also illustrates the difference between methodological precedent and empirical validation. In an evaluation with 177 volunteer undergraduate and graduate engineers, agreement between the generated recommendation and each participant’s current discipline was only slight (Cohen’s κ = 0.23; the article reports a statistically significant result) [1]. The authors state that the small evaluation sample affected agreement and propose increasing it. More fundamentally, the outcome was agreement with participants’ existing disciplines—not prospective student success, career attainment, or a causal effect of the recommendation. The association sample was drawn from volunteers at a single university, and the authors note that the fuzzy rules lacked a specific standard for translating human experience into rules and that membership functions required tuning. They also identify adding other majors and socioeconomic factors as future work [1]. These constraints caution against transferring PCRS’s inputs, rules, weights, or apparent agreement to CareerInsight AI.

Lahoud and colleagues extend the foundation from one fuzzy recommender to a comparison of recommendation paradigms for university-major and career-domain guidance. Their study constructs a novel ontology and compares user-based and item-based collaborative filtering, demographic-based recommendation, knowledge-based recommendation supported by case-based reasoning, ontology-based methods, and hybridizations in a Lebanese high-school-student case study [2]. The reported knowledge-based hybrid combines user-based collaborative filtering with case-based reasoning and an ontology. Its abstract reports 98% similar cases, 95% of those cases personalized to students’ interests, average usefulness feedback of 95%, and satisfaction of 92.5% [2]. The ontology’s proposed reuse in other educational systems is especially relevant to CareerInsight AI’s need to represent structured relationships among user evidence, occupations, domains, and rationale components.

The comparison with PCRS is instructive: PCRS emphasizes explicit fuzzy rules and ranked suitability across engineering disciplines, whereas Lahoud et al. compare collaborative, demographic, case-based, ontology-based, and hybrid knowledge representations. However, the accessible abstract for the latter does not provide sample size, recruitment details, operational definitions for the reported percentages, evaluation protocol, or metric definitions; the institutional full-text endpoint was inaccessible during verification [2]. Its results therefore support the existence of a relevant hybrid/ontology precedent, not a transferable performance benchmark. The study is also a Lebanese high-school-student case study, so its population and setting should not be assumed to represent CareerInsight AI users.

Taken together, these career-domain studies support a design principle rather than a validated formula: a career recommender can combine heterogeneous profile evidence with domain knowledge and produce ranked options, but the representation, rule construction, and validation target must be explicit. PCRS supplies an example of interpretable rule-based aggregation; Lahoud et al. supply an example of ontology-supported and hybrid recommendation. Neither establishes which inputs, weights, or recommendation outcomes are appropriate for CareerInsight AI.

## Explainability and evidence-grounded rationale

Zhang and Chen provide conceptual grounding for separating the explanation shown to a user from the algorithmic mechanism that produces a recommendation. Their survey organizes explainable recommendation through the 5W framework, traces the research chronologically, and proposes a two-dimensional taxonomy crossing the information source or display style of an explanation with the algorithmic mechanism used to generate it [5]. It distinguishes explanations generated post hoc from explanations arising directly from an explainable, interpretable, or transparent model, and discusses possible aims including transparency, persuasiveness, effectiveness, trustworthiness, satisfaction, and system debugging [5]. For CareerInsight AI, this distinction means that a readable rationale—such as why a role follows from profile or labor-market evidence—is not by itself proof that the underlying scoring mechanism is interpretable or valid.

The survey covers product, social, and point-of-interest recommendation rather than career recommendation, and its accessible verified text is the publisher/author abstract. It does not report an empirical test of CareerInsight AI, a career-domain recommender, or causal evidence that explanations improve outcomes [5]. Its review coverage concerns research published in or before 2018, so it is a conceptual foundation rather than a complete account of later explainability work [5]. Accordingly, its taxonomy can guide documentation of CareerInsight AI’s explanation source and generation mechanism, but it cannot justify the prototype’s explanation format or claims of user benefit.

Ariza-Casabona, Boratto, and Salamó make the grounding problem more concrete through a reproducibility-driven benchmark of text-based explainable recommender methods. They harmonize task formulation, preprocessing, target explanations, baselines, and metrics across generation-based, extraction-based, and hybrid methods, and report experiments on three real-world datasets [3]. They introduce a feature-hallucination perspective that checks whether explanation features are unsupported by item evidence [3]. Their framing is useful by analogy for CareerInsight AI: each explanation statement could be checked against the structured career evidence that is supposed to support it, separately from measures of clarity, usefulness, or text quality.

The comparison with Zhang and Chen is complementary. Zhang and Chen offer a taxonomy for understanding what an explanation is and how it is generated; Ariza-Casabona et al. emphasize reproducible comparison and whether textual claims are grounded in available evidence. The latter study is nevertheless limited to text-based, review-driven explainable recommendation. Its benchmark does not establish validity for career recommendations or structured career evidence, and the verified excerpts do not demonstrate user studies or downstream trust improvements [3]. The authors also identify reproducibility constraints, including unavailable source code for some candidate works, resource exclusions, and differences in preprocessing and evaluation [3]. These limitations reinforce the need for CareerInsight AI to distinguish evidence support from user-perceived usefulness and from recommendation accuracy.

## Evaluation: beyond a single ranking metric

Bauer, Zangerle, and Said review evaluation practice across 57 recommender-systems papers published from 2017 to 2022, focusing on experiment types, datasets, and metrics [4]. They find that offline experimentation predominates; online evaluations are commonly combined with offline experiments. They also report concentration around a small number of datasets, notably MovieLens and Amazon review data, and around precision, normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain, and recall, while many other datasets and metrics appear infrequently [4]. Beyond-accuracy qualities are rarely assessed, leading the review to characterize evaluation as relatively narrow in its concentration on a few methods, datasets, and metrics [4].

For CareerInsight AI, this review supports an evaluation plan broader than offline ranking accuracy. Reporting should make evidence coverage and provenance visible, and may need to assess multiple dimensions separately: ranking or retrieval behavior, whether recommendation rationales are supported by the available evidence, and user-perceived usefulness or satisfaction. These are design implications from a general evaluation review, not evidence that any particular metric or user measure is correct for this prototype. The source’s scope is bounded to the 57-paper review and does not establish the effectiveness of CareerInsight AI, a career-specific system, an explanation method, or any scoring weights [4].

The five sources collectively suggest a layered evaluation logic. A system-design study such as PCRS can show that structured inputs and explicit rules are implementable, but agreement with current discipline is a narrow and potentially confounded outcome [1]. A comparative case study can indicate that a hybrid and ontology-based approach is promising in one setting, but reported percentages require protocol and sample details before they can be interpreted or reproduced [2]. Explainability research indicates that explanation type, generation mechanism, and evidence grounding should be distinguished [3][5]. Evaluation research cautions that relying on a small set of offline accuracy metrics can miss qualities that matter to users and system operators [4]. None of these layers, alone or in combination, validates CareerInsight AI.

## Research gap and implications for CareerInsight AI

A carefully qualified gap emerges at the intersection of these literatures: the verified sources provide precedents for profile-based career recommendation, ontology-supported hybrid guidance, explanation taxonomies, evidence-grounding checks, and broader recommender evaluation, but they do not establish a validated, career-specific protocol for a system that combines structured individual evidence with explicit, inspectable rationale and evaluates both recommendation behavior and explanation support. This is a design-and-validation gap, not proof that no such work exists. The present sources are heterogeneous in domain, population, evidence type, and evaluation disclosure, so absence from this five-source set should not be interpreted as a comprehensive absence from the field.

CareerInsight AI can address this gap only through its own future validation. A defensible program would pre-specify the evidence schema and weights, document how evidence maps to scores and explanations, test whether each rationale is supported by stored evidence, and evaluate recommendations against an appropriate outcome rather than treating current discipline or immediate satisfaction as a sufficient proxy. It should report sample and recruitment details, compare against suitable baselines, and separate ranking quality, explanation quality or grounding, and user-perceived usefulness. Any such evaluation would be new evidence about CareerInsight AI; it cannot be inferred from the sources reviewed here.
